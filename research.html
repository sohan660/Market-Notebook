What do these numbers mean?

A research tool that teaches as you explore.

THE BIG IDEA

Look for trends across several years, not just a single impressive number. Revenue growth is helpful only when you understand how it turns into profit.

Revenue is the money a company makes from selling products or services before expenses are deducted.
THINK LIKE AN ANALYST

Questions worth asking

There is more to a company than an upward-sloping chart.

How does this business actually make its money?
Are sales and profits growing consistently?
What could cause this investment to lose value?
YOUR NEXT STEP

Turn curiosity into research.

Build a habit of checking the numbers, understanding the business, and writing down the risks.

SIDE BY SIDE

Put two companies under the microscope.

Compare reported revenue, profitability and earnings for the same fiscal-year label. Period end dates are shown because fiscal years do not cover identical months. EPS alone does not measure valuation.

HOW TO THINK ABOUT IT

Bigger isn't always better.

A company with more revenue isn't necessarily the better investment. Pay attention to the business model, debt, valuation, and consistency.

  • Compare the same periods.
  • Look at margins, not just sales.
  • Consider different industries.
  • Write down what you still don't know.

Historical sources and calculation notes

This is a fixed educational snapshot, not a live market-data service or an ongoing filing tracker. Apple: FY2024–2026. Microsoft: FY2024–2026. Coca-Cola: FY2023–2025. Later releases are intentionally excluded. Figures are reported GAAP, not adjusted earnings.

Revenue and net income are stored in USD millions and displayed as billions. Net income is attributable to company shareholders (excluding noncontrolling interests for Coca-Cola). Net margin = net income ÷ revenue × 100. Revenue growth compares consecutive fiscal years. Diluted EPS comes directly from the report and is not a share price. No P/E is shown because this snapshot has no dated market-price feed.

Read the original statements for one-time items and accounting changes before comparing companies. These figures are educational and do not determine whether a stock is a good investment.
