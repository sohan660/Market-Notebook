Investing 101

This is the guide I wish I'd had: what a stock is, what an ETF is, what fees do, and what the market has really done. It's for learning, not a recommendation.

Before you invest anything

Rent, groceries and next semester's bill shouldn't sit in the stock market, because prices can drop right when you need the cash. Keep some money in a plain savings account first. Also look at any high-interest debt before investing. Paying off a credit card charging 20% or more beats hoping for a 10% market return.

What's a stock?

Say your favorite coffee shop went public. If you bought one share, you'd own a tiny slice of the shop. If the business does well, the share price can rise, and some companies also pay dividends. If it does badly, the price can fall and you can lose some or all of what you put in. A famous brand isn't automatically a good buy at today's price.

What's an ETF?

ETF stands for exchange-traded fund. It's a basket of investments that trades like a single stock. A broad stock ETF can hold hundreds or thousands of companies, so one bad company hurts less. It can still lose money when the whole market falls. And not every ETF is broad: some hold one industry or one country, which is a lot riskier. Look at what's inside before you buy.

Fees: small numbers, big difference

A fee of 1% sounds like nothing. It isn't, because it comes out every single year and also takes away what that money would have earned. The Investment Company Institute says equity mutual funds charged 1.04% on average in 1996 and 0.40% in 2025, and index equity ETFs averaged 0.14% in 2025. The SEC's own example: $100,000 growing 4% a year for 20 years ends up roughly $30,000 lower with a 1% fee than with a 0.25% fee. Try your own numbers:

Where do you actually buy this stuff?

  1. Pick a goal. Retirement decades away, or something closer?
  2. Pick an account. A regular brokerage account and a Roth IRA follow different tax rules. A Roth IRA needs earned income (like wages from a job), so check the current rules first.
  3. Compare brokerages. Look at fees, what you can buy, and whether the firm is registered. FINRA BrokerCheck is free.
  4. Read what you're buying. For an ETF, the fund page and expense ratio. For a stock, the company's financial reports.
  5. Start with a habit. Putting in a set amount regularly (dollar-cost averaging) builds discipline. It doesn't guarantee a profit.

Green flags and red flags

Green: short-term money stays in savings. You know what the fees are. You're spread across many companies. You checked the claim against a real source.

Red: "guaranteed 20% returns." Buying because of a viral post. Assuming famous investors can't lose. Putting rent money into volatile stuff.
