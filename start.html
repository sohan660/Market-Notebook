Before stocks: keep your everyday money safe.

If you might need money for rent, groceries or next semester's bill, it probably doesn't belong in stocks. The market can drop right before you need to pay something. Think about emergency savings and high-interest debt before deciding how much you can invest.

A better starting point: Know what the money is for and when you'll need it. You can learn about investing without opening an account today.
Lesson 01

What's a stock?

A share of stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. When the business does well, its shares may become more valuable. But the stock price can also fall even when the company is making money.

How could you make money?

If the share price rises, your investment becomes more valuable. Some companies also pay dividends—cash distributions to shareholders.

What could go wrong?

The business could disappoint, face new competitors, or lose customers. Stock prices can fall, and you could lose some or all of what you invested.

A stock is not a guaranteed paycheck. Owning a familiar brand doesn't mean its shares are a good price.

Lesson 02

What's an ETF?

ETF stands for exchange-traded fund. Instead of buying one business, you buy shares of a fund that holds investments according to a defined strategy. Many ETFs hold dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of stocks.

Think of it this way: A stock gives you a piece of one company. A broad ETF can give you small pieces of many companies in one purchase. That reduces some company-specific risk, but it doesn't protect you from an overall market decline.

ETFs aren't all the same: some track the broad market, while others focus on one industry, one country or even more complicated trading strategies. The word “ETF” alone does not mean safe or diversified.

Try a real-world check

How I'd research an ETF in five minutes

Before deciding whether an ETF interests you, open the fund company's official page and look for these five things. You don't need to buy anything to practice.

Look at the fund itself

  1. What index or strategy? Does it track the broad U.S. stock market, one sector, or something else?
  2. What does it hold? Check the top ten holdings. Is one company or industry a huge share?
  3. What's the expense ratio? It's an annual cost built into a fund's returns. Lower isn't automatically better, but costs deserve attention.

Then check the fit

  1. How risky is it? Compare its objective and risks with when you'd need your money.
  2. How does it trade? ETFs trade during market hours; the bid-ask spread can add a cost when buying or selling.

Also check your brokerage's fees and the fund's official prospectus. A ticker symbol alone won't tell you enough.

Try comparing two fund pages without buying either one. Which has broader holdings? Which is cheaper? Can you explain each in one sentence?

Lesson 03

How do you actually get started?

Usually through an account at a regulated brokerage. In the U.S., a standard taxable brokerage account and a Roth IRA are different account types with different tax rules. A Roth IRA generally requires eligible earned income and is designed for long-term retirement savings. Learn the current eligibility rules before choosing an account.

  1. Set a goal. Is the money for retirement decades away or something closer?
  2. Research the account. Compare fees, withdrawal rules, investment choices and whether the firm is properly registered.
  3. Understand the investment. Read an ETF's fund page and prospectus or a company's financial information.
  4. Know your costs. Check expense ratios, commissions, spreads and other charges.
  5. Start with a system, not a prediction. Regular contributions can build a habit, but dollar-cost averaging does not guarantee a profit or prevent loss.

See the SEC's investor education guides and FINRA BrokerCheck before relying on a financial firm or professional.

Lesson 04

Common mistakes to avoid

The good habits

  • Keep short-term money accessible.
  • Understand fees before buying.
  • Consider diversification across investments.
  • Check information against primary sources.
  • Be realistic about risk and time horizon.

The red flags

  • “Guaranteed 20% returns.”
  • Buying based only on a viral post.
  • Assuming famous investors cannot lose money.
  • Putting rent money into volatile securities.
  • Ignoring taxes, spreads and expense ratios.

You don't have to predict the next big stock to learn how investing works. Start by understanding the investment, the risk, and the cost.
