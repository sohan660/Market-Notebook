What the famous investors actually own

I pulled numbers from SEC filings for Berkshire Hathaway (Buffett's company), Pershing Square (Ackman) and Soros Fund Management, then added John Bogle as the "just buy the index" counterpoint. Data is as of mid-2026.

Read this first: a 13F filing covers certain reportable securities at quarter-end, generally filed within 45 days. It can include shares, options and convertible securities. The dollar figures are what the firm holds, not anyone's personal wealth or profit, and they leave out cash, most shorts and private deals. Use them to ask questions, not as a shopping list.

Warren Buffett and Berkshire Hathaway

Buffett took control of Berkshire in 1965 and ran it as CEO until the end of 2025, when Greg Abel took over. His style is simple to describe: buy businesses you understand at a fair price, then hold for a very long time.

That's a remarkable historical record. It's also not a number to plug into your own plan, and it didn't happen smoothly:

In these 11 years Berkshire beat the index 6 times and lagged it 5 times, including −12.5% in 2015 and big gaps in 2019, 2020, 2023 and 2025. Even Buffett's company sat out some of the rallies.

Those four companies made up about 63% of its disclosed equity portfolio in this snapshot. Berkshire also owns whole companies like GEICO and BNSF Railway, which don't show up here at all.

My takeaway: look at what a business is worth, not whether it's trending. Also: even an exceptionally successful company can trail the index for long stretches. A few years of returns don't settle a strategy's merits.

Bill Ackman and Pershing Square

Ackman runs a concentrated portfolio and sometimes pushes the companies he owns to change. For June 30, 2026, the firm's holdings were reported under Pershing Square Inc., with $19.47 billion across 15 filing rows (14 distinct securities; Howard Hughes appears in two rows). Alphabet was absent from that quarter-end 13F disclosure.

My takeaway: betting big on a few ideas can pay off a lot, or hurt a lot. Fourteen positions is not the same risk as 500.

George Soros and Soros Fund Management

Soros is known for macro investing and a theory he calls reflexivity: what investors believe can move prices, and those price moves change what investors believe. The 13F below is for the firm he founded, so read it as a big team's portfolio, not George's personal picks.

The original filing reports 266 information-table entries with a total value of $8,143,628,824 ($8.14 billion). This includes every instrument type in the filing. Entries are not the same as unique companies, and reported option values are not option premiums or a measure of net economic exposure. We use the original filing total consistently for all three managers.

John Bogle and the index fund

Bogle founded Vanguard, which launched its first index mutual fund for regular investors in 1976. The idea: instead of paying someone to pick winners, own a broad slice of the market cheaply. It can still lose money. What you control is the cost, which is why the fee calculator matters. Vanguard: 50 years of indexing

What I'd take from all of this

Buffett says study the business and be patient. Ackman shows what conviction looks like, and what it risks. Soros says psychology moves markets. Bogle says don't pick, and keep your costs low. They disagree, and each has years where they looked wrong. None of it is a reason to copy someone's portfolio.
