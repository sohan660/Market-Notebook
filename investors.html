The investor playbook · November 2026

What can we actually learn from famous investors?

There are lots of posts about what Buffett, Ackman or Soros just bought. I wanted to look at a different question: how do they think about investing, and what can their public filings actually tell us?

Read this first: The portfolio dollar amounts below are reported values of disclosed securities positions for investment firms. They are not the manager's personal profits, net worth, client returns, or evidence that someone turned $100,000 into billions. GuruFocus summarizes filings. These examples are historical snapshots, not live holdings.
Three different approaches

What their strategies have in common—and where they differ

The useful part isn't copying a ticker. It's understanding how a decision was made.

Value + patience

Warren Buffett

Associated with buying businesses he understands, seeking durable advantages, and holding for the long term. Berkshire also acquires and operates whole businesses, not just listed stocks.

The lesson: Ask what a business is worth, not just whether the stock is trending.

Concentration + activism

Bill Ackman

Pershing Square has used a relatively concentrated portfolio and activist engagement—sometimes arguing that companies can improve their operations or capital allocation.

$13.71BGuruFocus-reported Pershing Square disclosed 13F equity portfolio value · March 31, 2026 · 11 holdings

The lesson: Having a strong thesis means understanding both the upside and how it can go wrong. Concentration also increases risk.

Macro + market psychology

George Soros

Known for macro investing and the idea of reflexivity: investor beliefs can affect prices, and prices can feed back into beliefs and decisions.

$6.58BGuruFocus-reported Soros Fund Management disclosed 13F equity portfolio value · June 30, 2026 · 252 holdings

The lesson: Market narratives and emotions matter—but rapid, leveraged or macro trading can be especially risky.

If you're looking at a GuruFocus profile yourself, first check the portfolio date, the top holdings as a percentage, and whether the page is showing 13F positions rather than audited returns. That's a better starting point than copying the biggest ticker.

Another approach worth knowing

John Bogle: what if you owned the whole market instead?

Bogle, Vanguard's founder, helped make low-cost index investing accessible to individuals. Vanguard launched its first index mutual fund for individual investors in 1976. The idea was simple: rather than trying to consistently beat the market with a few picks, own a broad slice of it at relatively low cost.

What makes this different?

An index fund generally follows a benchmark by rule. It doesn't need a star stock picker to decide which company will win next quarter.

What still matters?

It can still lose money. The index's holdings, diversification, expense ratio and your investing time horizon all matter. Indexing is a strategy, not a guarantee.

A research caution

Why you can't simply copy a guru's portfolio

What 13F reports show

U.S. institutional investment managers above certain thresholds disclose selected securities positions quarterly. GuruFocus organizes some of this public filing information into readable profiles.

What 13F reports don't show

A full real-time portfolio, a manager's exact investment thesis, every type of asset or hedge, private investments, all short exposure, or personal investment gains. Filings can arrive up to 45 days after each quarter ends.

This is why a holdings table should be a starting point for questions, not a list of stocks to buy. Ask what business the investor saw, what risks existed, and what facts might have changed since the report.

Here's what I'd take from these examples

What seems useful for beginners

Buffett reminds us to study businesses and be patient. Ackman shows how much conviction and activism can shape a concentrated strategy. Soros suggests that market psychology matters. Bogle offers a compelling argument for broad, low-cost indexing instead of chasing a manager's next pick.

Those are different paths. A college student shouldn't assume that a large institutional portfolio, a famous name, or one strong historical return means a particular strategy is right for them.
