A BETTER WAY TO LEARN

Don't copy an investor. Build your own process.

It is tempting to ask what a famous investor bought this quarter. A better starting point is learning how to ask useful questions before you invest.

Here are four ideas that can help whether you prefer diversified funds or studying individual companies. No live holdings, stock picks, or data that needs a filing-season refresh.

Explore the four principles ↓

THE FOUNDATIONS

Four habits worth learning

01 / UNDERSTANDING

Know what you own.

Can you explain how a business makes money in two sentences? If not, start there. A recognizable brand is not automatically a good investment.

Ask yourself: Who pays this company, and why?

02 / RISK

Don't bet everything on one idea.

Diversification spreads company-specific risk, but it cannot stop an entire market from falling. Keep money you need soon out of risky assets.

Ask yourself: What happens if I am wrong?

03 / COSTS

Pay attention to the quiet fees.

Costs compound too. A small annual fee difference can mean a lot over decades, particularly with regular contributions.

Ask yourself: What am I paying, and what do I get?

04 / PATIENCE

Have a reason to stay invested.

A headline or a red trading day is not necessarily a change in the long-term case. Decide what evidence would change your mind before emotions take over.

Ask yourself: What would make me reconsider?

DIFFERENT ROUTES, SAME QUESTIONS

Two common approaches

The diversified-fund approach

Rather than choosing individual winners, invest broadly and pay attention to costs. John Bogle helped popularize low-cost index investing.

Broad funds still rise and fall, and different ETFs can have very different exposures.

Learn how ETFs work →

The business-research approach

Study a company's products, competitive position, financial history, price and risks. Warren Buffett is widely associated with long-term business ownership and fundamental analysis.

Even a great business may be an unwise purchase at the wrong price.

TRY IT YOURSELF

A quick thinking exercise

Your favorite company announces excellent sales. Does that automatically mean its stock is a good buy?

See a better way to think about it

Not necessarily. You would still want to look at profit, debt, competitors, future expectations and what investors already pay for each share. A business can be improving while its stock is priced too optimistically.

The goal isn't to predict tomorrow's price. It's to ask a better question.

NEXT STEP

Make your own research checklist.

Pick a business, review the financial history, write down the risks and record what you still need to learn. Your notes stay in your browser.
