Charts & data

Three charts I wish every new investor understood.

A chart is useful when you know where the numbers came from. These three show how time, market performance and fees can change an investing story.

Data checked November 2026 · Real historical returns are labeled separately from hypothetical examples.

01 · Model, not market history

What if you invested $25 every week?

Imagine putting away $25 each week instead of trying to find a winning stock. I turned that into a monthly contribution of $108.33 and used the same assumed return for every year. Here's what the math shows.

What to notice: At 10 years, most of the balance is your own contributions. By 30 years, the assumed growth is a larger part of the result. The risk is that real returns will not look this smooth.

For example, in 2022 the S&P 500 fell 18.1%, while Berkshire's share value rose 4.0%. But that doesn't mean Berkshire is always safer: its share value fell 31.8% in 2008, according to the same report.

What to notice: One good year doesn't establish a winning strategy. Look at the down years too. An annual return chart tells you what happened, not what will happen next.

Source: Berkshire Hathaway 2025 Annual Report, page 20. Data reproduced as an original small teaching graphic, not a live market chart.

03 · SEC educational example

How much can a small fee matter?

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission gives a useful example: start with $100,000, assume 4% annual growth before fees, and leave it invested for 20 years. Only the annual fee changes.

Value after 20 years

0.25% annual fee
$208,000
0.50% annual fee
$198,000
1.00% annual fee
$179,000

Approximate outcomes from the SEC's educational model, not actual investments. Illustrative 4% annual return before fees; 20-year holding period.

What to notice: The 1.00% fee scenario ends around $29,000 lower than the 0.25% scenario. Checking an ETF's expense ratio takes a minute; over time, costs can add up.

Source: SEC Investor.gov, “Understanding Fees.” The values—approximately $208,000, $198,000 and $179,000—are from the SEC illustration.

How I check an investing chart

  1. What is being measured? A stock price, a percentage return, or a hypothetical balance?
  2. What dates does it cover? One great year and twenty years of results are not the same thing.
  3. Where is the source? Look for a filing, fund report or credible investor education source.
  4. What is left out? Dividends, fees, taxes, inflation, losses and risk can change the story.

The lesson isn't to copy a chart. It's to get comfortable asking what the chart is actually saying.
