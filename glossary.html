THE SMALL DICTIONARY I WISH I HAD

Finance words, without the jargon.

Sometimes the hardest part of learning investing is knowing what people are talking about. I put the terms I kept seeing into one place, with short explanations and examples.

These are simplified learning definitions, not investing recommendations. Search works on this page without an account or a data feed.

29 of 29 terms

The basics01

Stock

A small piece of ownership in a company. If the business does well, that does not automatically mean its stock price goes up.

Think: you own a slice of a business, not a guaranteed paycheck.

The basics02

Share

One unit of stock or another investment. Share prices change as people buy and sell.

Owning 2 shares is not the same as owning 2% of a company.

The basics03

Ticker symbol

The short code used to identify a publicly traded security.

AAPL is Apple's stock ticker. Different securities can have similar names.

The basics04

Brokerage account

An account through which you can buy, sell and hold investments.

Check fees, account rules and protections before opening one.

The basics05

Portfolio

The investments you own as a group, not just one favorite stock.

A portfolio can include stocks, funds, bonds and cash.

The basics06

Index

A measurement tracking a defined group of investments. An index itself isn't something you directly own.

The S&P 500 is an index; funds can try to follow it.

Funds & fees07

ETF

An exchange-traded fund is a basket of investments that trades on an exchange like a stock.

A broad ETF can hold hundreds of companies; a narrow ETF might hold only one sector.

Funds & fees08

Index fund

A fund designed to follow an index rather than pick winners one by one.

Check what the index includes, what the fund costs and how closely it tracks.

Funds & fees09

Mutual fund

A pooled investment fund. Unlike an ETF, a typical mutual fund is bought and sold at its end-of-day net asset value.

Both mutual funds and ETFs can be broad or very focused.

Funds & fees10

Expense ratio

The annual operating cost of a fund, expressed as a percentage of fund assets.

A 0.10% expense ratio is about $10 per year for every $10,000 invested, before changes in value.

Risk11

Diversification

Spreading money across holdings so one company's trouble matters less.

It can reduce company-specific risk, but it can't guarantee against market losses.

Risk12

Volatility

How much an investment's price moves around. Big price swings can feel exciting or stressful.

Volatility is not identical to the chance of permanent loss, but it matters when you need money soon.

Risk13

Risk tolerance

How much uncertainty and potential loss you can handle financially and emotionally.

Your timeline matters: tuition money due soon is different from money saved for decades.

Risk14

Asset allocation

How you divide investments among asset categories such as stocks, bonds and cash.

Allocation decisions often matter more than choosing a single hot stock.

Risk15

Bond

A type of loan to a company or government. Bonds can pay interest and return principal at maturity, but can lose value and carry risk.

Bond prices often fall when market interest rates rise.

Risk16

Liquidity

How easily an asset can be sold for cash without moving its price much.

A heavily traded ETF may be easier to sell than a thinly traded stock.

The basics17

Compound growth

Growth on your original money and on earlier growth. Compounding can help over long periods, but investments can also decline.

The rate and number of years matter. Try the calculator in Investing 101.

The basics18

Dollar-cost averaging

Investing a set amount at regular intervals, regardless of the market price.

It builds a habit, but doesn't eliminate the risk of losses.

Research19

Dividend

A payment a company may make to its shareholders, often from profits or available cash.

Dividends are not guaranteed. A company can reduce or stop them.

Research20

Dividend yield

Annual dividend per share divided by the current share price, usually shown as a percentage.

A high yield can come from a falling share price; it isn't automatically a bargain.

Research21

Revenue

Money earned from selling goods or services before subtracting expenses.

Sales can rise even while profits fall.

Research22

Net income

The profit left after costs and taxes, under the accounting rules being used.

Compare several years and check for unusual one-time events.

Research23

EPS

Earnings per share: a company's earnings divided by a share-count measure. Diluted EPS accounts for potential additional shares.

EPS is not the stock price. It helps describe profit per share.

Research24

P/E ratio

Price-to-earnings ratio: stock price divided by earnings per share for a specified period.

A high P/E may reflect high expectations, not necessarily good value.

Research25

Market capitalization

A company's stock price multiplied by its shares outstanding.

This measures the market value of the equity, not the total value of everything the company owns.

Research26

Cash flow

Money moving into and out of a business. Cash flow can tell a different story from accounting profit.

A company can report profit yet still need cash to run operations.

Trading27

Market order

An instruction to trade promptly at the best available price. The execution price is not guaranteed.

In a fast market, you might pay more or receive less than you expected.

Trading28

Limit order

An instruction to buy no higher than a set price, or sell no lower than a set price. Execution isn't guaranteed.

A limit helps control price but the order might never fill.

Trading29

Bid-ask spread

The difference between the highest price a buyer currently offers and the lowest price a seller currently asks.

Wider spreads can make a trade more expensive.
